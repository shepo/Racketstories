Every club I've been involved with has had a waiting list at some point, and every one of them has had the same reason for it. The courts are full.

Ours has 43 people on it. Some have been on it since before the pandemic. One of them, I'm fairly sure, has since moved to Portugal.

So on a Tuesday last month I took a flask and a folding chair and went to look at the full courts.

![A lone player above empty courts](saturday-cartoon "Tuesday, eleven in the morning. The full courts.")

<!--more-->

At ten there was nobody. There were two pigeons on court four, which I haven't counted, as they aren't members.

At eleven a retired couple played singles on court one, quite slowly, for an hour, and left. That's one court out of six. A sixth full. Or, as it's described at committee, full.

Lunchtime, nobody. At two, a coach with one junior who probably should have been at school. I didn't ask. Not my department.

At three, nobody. Half three, a man came to look for a water bottle he'd left on Sunday. He found it. He didn't play.

From six it was a different place. All six courts going, people on the bench waiting, a doubles four disputing whose booking it was. When the lights went off everyone walked to the car park saying how busy the club is.

And they're right. It is busy. For about three and a half hours on four evenings a week, and Saturday morning. The rest of the time it's a very well kept field.

I raised this at the next meeting. I said, as near as I can remember, that we have 43 people who would like to give us money to play tennis, and a great many courts with nobody on them, and that these two facts might be connected.

The response, broadly:

- if we let them in, they'll all want Thursday at seven
- a waiting list is a sign of a healthy club
- we've always had one

The first may well be true. But that's a Thursday problem, not a membership problem, and there are ways round a Thursday problem. More sessions. A second social on Tuesday. Half the people on at half six and half at eight. You don't fix a busy hour by turning away people who would happily play at eleven in the morning. Some of those 43 are retired. Some work shifts. One, I'm told, is a nurse who is only free on weekday afternoons, which is precisely when the courts are at their fullest of pigeons.

The second I've given some thought. A waiting list is a sign that people want to join. It isn't a sign of anything else. A queue outside a shop that's shut most of the day is not a healthy shop. It's a shop with a queue.

The third is accurate. We have always had one. We also always had the old tea urn, and that went in 2019 to general relief.

We agreed to look at it again in the spring, by which time the list will be fifty and the courts will still be empty at eleven.

I stayed on court three with my flask until four, partly to make a point and partly because it was a nice afternoon. Nobody asked me to move. Nobody needed it.

P.S. The man in Portugal has written to ask whether he's moved up. He has. Two people ahead of him gave up.
