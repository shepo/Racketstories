It's my third week at Tuesday mixers. I've *finally* started to get the hang of it. I can hold a rally! Sometimes even four shots! And then the draw puts me on court two.

My partner is lovely. The two on the other side are not messing about.

<!--more-->

The first serve goes past me before I've lifted my racket. The next one hits the back fence. By the fourth game I'm quietly hoping the ball goes anywhere other than towards me, which of course it doesn't: a volley thumped at me from about three feet away. I made a noise I didn't know I could make.

![Pixel-art tennis game with level-up icons](level-up "How I imagine my progress looks. How it actually looks: me apologising.")

## The soundtrack

And then there's the *soundtrack*. "Sorry!" from the player who just fired it past me. "Sorry!" from me, for missing it. "Sorry, I'll ease off." "No, no, sorry, my fault." Half the conversation on court is people apologising for a mismatch none of them chose.

I counted on Tuesday. Forty-six sorries from me, across two hours. (I like counting things. It's a coping mechanism.)

It's not just tennis. My friend who got me into padel says it's the same there: you're pinned against the back glass while the other two take turns sending the ball off every wall. My cousin plays squash and says it's every shot hammered down the wall, past you, while you stand in the middle like a lamp post.

And it's not only the beginner who suffers. Last week I watched one of the strong players on our court ease right off, serving at half pace and hoping for a return, and it clipped the frame of my partner's racket and vanished into the dark. Every rally was over in two shots. He looked as miserable as I felt.

## Nobody did anything wrong

Here's what I've worked out after three months: **nobody did anything wrong.** The person who hit that smash wasn't being mean. They were just playing how they always play. The problem wasn't the people. It was the *match*.

Nobody should have to say sorry on court. Not for hitting a winner, and not for being new. That's why racket sports need some kind of ability measure. Not for bragging rights, but so nobody has the worst evening of their week at what was supposed to be a *social*.

## The squeeze in the middle

Graham (who writes on here and is very good at explaining things over a pint) told me that in any club, most people sit in the middle. Very few are right at the top, very few right at the bottom. Which sounds like it should make matching easy, until you remember that "intermediate" covers a *huge* range, and the people at the edges, like me, are the ones most likely to have a rubbish time.

So a good level system is really doing two jobs:

- **Protecting beginners.** Starting tennis or padel is brilliant. But it only becomes a hobby if the first few sessions are fun. A beginner who gets games they can compete in comes back next week. A beginner who gets peppered doesn't. (I nearly didn't.)
- **Keeping strong players happy.** They want a proper game too. Put them with the right people and they stay, and often they're happy to bring newer players along with them.

Get both right and everybody wins.

## All the numbers

Every racket sport has its own way of putting a number on you, and I've spent an alarming amount of time this month reading about them. Tennis has had NTRP in the US, ITN internationally, and now the World Tennis Number and UTR. Squash has grades, club ladders and SquashLevels. Padel and pickleball have their own (DUPR for pickleball, Playtomic levels for padel). Walk into any club and someone will tell you they're "a 3.5" or "a B-grade" or "about a 4". I am, I'm told, "about a 1. Ish."

They all exist for the same very human reason: *how do we stop mismatched games being miserable for everyone?*

## When winning matters, and when it doesn't

In competition, the better player should win. Knockouts, leagues, ladders: the scoreboard is the most honest judge there is.

But most of us aren't playing competition most of the time. We're at a Tuesday social or a Sunday morning mixer. And there, the number matters for a different reason. **It isn't about who wins. It's about whether the game is fun, fair and close.** A great social game has long rallies, and everyone gets a chance to hit a winner and everyone gets stretched.

## What my club did about it

Our club runs its sessions on Rallio, and last week they turned on a new thing called Rallio Levels. I was sceptical, because I assumed it would just be another number to feel bad about. It's actually quite clever:

- You can **bring your own rating** (World Tennis Number, UTR, DUPR, Playtomic level, SquashLevels), or
- Use the **club's own levels**. Ours are called things like "Improvers" and "Thursday Group 2", which is very us.
- Your **level moves as you play**. Ladder, box league and competition matches count fully. Mixers and open games count for half, so one off night at a social won't knock you down a band. Which, given Tuesday, is a relief.

The bit that matters for me: when Bev builds the mixer draw now, she can see everyone's level at a glance and balance the courts, so there's no court two of doom before a ball's even been hit. The stronger players get mixed in with people still finding their feet, instead of all ending up on one court together.

Apparently clubs can also stretch the bands to fit the players they actually have, so "intermediate" becomes a few proper steps rather than one giant bucket. Our strongest member is a lovely retired PE teacher, not a professional, so this makes sense even to me.

## The short version

Levels matter. But the prize isn't the number. It's the good game the number helps you find. It's the new player who comes back for week four (that's me, I came back), the strong player who's happy to bring them along, and a court where nobody has to say sorry.

Next Tuesday's target: under thirty sorries. I'll report back.

P.S. My partner from court two messaged afterwards to say "you'll be fine, we all started there". I was not fine. But it was very kind.
