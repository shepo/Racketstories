Last October, at the club AGM, someone said "we should have a ladder" and I said "I could do that, it's just a spreadsheet". Reader, it was not just a spreadsheet.

I ran our club ladder by hand for seven months. Here's everything I learned, mostly the hard way, so you don't have to.

## First, what a ladder is

A tennis ladder is a ranked list of players. You go up by beating people above you. That's it. It gives a group a reason to keep arranging matches without a fixed schedule, and it suits clubs where people want competition but can't promise to turn up to a league night every week.

The lovely thing is it never stops. No season to wait for, no fixtures to set. Want a game? Challenge someone.

## Ladder or box league?

Before you start, it's worth knowing the other option, because half our committee wanted a box league instead and we argued about it for a whole meeting (Graham took minutes; they're very thorough).

In a **box league**, players are split into small boxes of four to six people of a similar standard. Over a set period, usually four to six weeks, everyone in a box plays everyone else. Points get added up, the top players go up a box, the bottom ones go down, new boxes form, repeat.

Here's my honest take on both.

**Good things about a ladder**

- It runs all the time, with no fixed period or fixtures
- You pick when to play and who to challenge, so it's low commitment
- New people can join whenever
- It's easy to explain and quick to start

**Bad things about a ladder**

- It relies on people actually issuing challenges, so a quiet group can stall
- People can sit on a position and dodge challenges (you know who you are, Phil)
- Nobody is guaranteed a set number of matches
- Quieter players can go weeks without a game

**Good things about a box league**

- Guaranteed matches, because you play everyone in your box
- Grouped by standard, so games stay close
- Promotion and relegation give you a proper sense of progress
- Small boxes are sociable and you get to know people

**Bad things about a box league**

- Every match has to be arranged and played inside the period
- Harder to join halfway through
- Much more to set up and track: making boxes, recording results, working out who goes up and down
- Unplayed matches mess up the final table

Short version: a ladder rewards people who get on with it and suits a flexible, informal group. A box league guarantees games and structure but costs more admin. We went with a ladder. The rest of this is about that.

## Step 1: Pick your format

Most clubs run a **challenge ladder**. You challenge people ranked near you and swap places based on the result. It works for singles, and for doubles where pairs hold a spot together.

The big decision is the **challenge range**: how far up you're allowed to challenge. Two or three places above you keeps games competitive and stops the person at the bottom challenging straight to the top. Decide this *before* you start. I didn't, and the first week was chaos.

## Step 2: Write the rules down

A ladder lives or dies by its rules. Agree them up front and make them visible to everyone:

- **Challenge range**: how many places above you can challenge
- **Response window**: how long someone has to accept and arrange a challenge
- **Scoring**: best of three sets, one set, or a match tiebreak, and how it gets recorded
- **What happens on a result**: normally the winner takes the higher spot and the loser drops below
- **What happens if a challenge is ignored**: usually it expires, or the person ignoring it gives up the spot

Vague rules cause arguments, and arguments land on the organiser, at 10.30pm, in capital letters. Clear rules settle most of them before they start.

## Step 3: Where it all went wrong (the spreadsheet bit)

The format is simple. The bookkeeping is not.

Someone has to log every challenge, record every result, reorder the table, chase the people who've gone quiet, and expire challenges nobody answered. On a shared spreadsheet plus a WhatsApp group, that someone was me, and it never stopped. I was updating rankings on my phone at a wedding. *During the speeches.*

And when I missed a couple of updates (holiday, flu, life), the rankings stopped matching reality, people stopped trusting them, lost interest, and the whole thing very nearly died in March.

The problem was never the ladder. It was the manual upkeep.

## Step 4: Let the ladder run itself

This is the bit worth handing over to a computer. A ladder should reorder itself the second a result goes in, and do the chasing you'd otherwise do yourself.

We moved ours to Rallio in April (made by Jake, who's a member here). Someone challenges anyone in their range, the two of you agree a time, and the winner puts the score in. The rankings reorder themselves, so the table's always current. Challenges nobody answers expire on their own, which keeps things moving without me policing it. It does singles or doubles, and people get a nudge when they're challenged or their position changes.

Setting it up took about a minute: make a group, pick tennis, invite people with a link or add them by name. They claim a profile with a tap, and there's no sign-up form in the way before they can challenge someone. Which mattered, because a few of our older members would rather lose 6-0 than fill in a form.

## Step 5: Keep it going

Even a ladder that runs itself needs a bit of a push now and again:

- Set a clear response window so challenges don't just sit there
- Encourage people to *issue* challenges rather than wait to be challenged
- Make the current standings easy to see so people can track their climb

The app handles the standings and the nudges, which gets rid of most of the reasons a ladder goes stale. I just do the encouraging now. Mostly by shouting "CHALLENGE SOMEONE" across the car park.
